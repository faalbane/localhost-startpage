AI is deeply embedded in how I work every day, both as a builder and as a user. On the engineering side, I use AI coding assistants (Claude, Copilot) for rapid prototyping, debugging, and exploring unfamiliar codebases. At CopyCatch.AI, our entire product is built on AI/ML pipelines for content authentication, so I am constantly working with LLMs, embeddings, and retrieval systems at the infrastructure level. I also use Perplexity daily as my primary research tool for technical questions, competitive analysis, and staying current on rapidly evolving AI developments.

One underappreciated benefit is how AI tools have compressed the feedback loop for technical decision-making. When I am evaluating architecture tradeoffs or researching an unfamiliar domain, I can go from question to informed opinion in minutes instead of hours. This does not replace deep expertise, but it dramatically accelerates the "exploration" phase of any problem. For a CTO managing multiple technical domains simultaneously, that compression is transformative.

The biggest pain point I see is context fragmentation. Every AI tool operates in its own silo. My coding assistant does not know what I researched in Perplexity, my search tool does not know what I am building in my IDE, and none of them maintain meaningful state across sessions. I end up re-explaining context repeatedly, which ironically creates friction in tools designed to reduce it. The AI tools that will win long term are the ones that solve this context problem, maintaining a coherent understanding of what I am working on across interactions and tools.

Another underappreciated issue is calibration. Current AI tools are often confidently wrong in ways that are hard to detect unless you already have domain expertise. In my healthcare work at Henry Ford Health System, where decisions had real patient impact, I learned that the cost of a confident wrong answer is much higher than the cost of an honest "I am not sure." The AI tools I trust most are the ones that surface their uncertainty and show their sources, which is exactly why Perplexity's citation-first approach stands out to me.
